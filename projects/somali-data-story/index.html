A data story

What 46,000 words reveal about Somali

I turned the standard Somali dictionary into data. Every headword, every sense, every cross reference: 46,314 entries in all. Once you can count them, a language starts to show its habits. Here is what I found.

By Abdullahi Mohamud Ahmed. Built on the dataset behind the Qaamuuska-NLP paper.

One

It begins with D

Sort every word by its first letter and one jumps out at once. More than six thousand entries start with D, with M and S trailing well behind. Somali writes only native sounds, so letters like P, V and Z never appear at the front of a word. What is left is a language that leans on a small set of opening consonants.

6,372 words begin with D. That is roughly one entry in seven.

Two

Most words are about eight letters long

Word length forms a clean hill. The typical headword runs six to nine letters and peaks right around eight. Very short words are rare. Very long ones, the compounds and technical coinages, thin out into a tail that stretches all the way to twenty six letters.

The average word is 8.2 letters long, and half are eight or shorter.

How many words have each length, in letters.

Three

Nouns and verbs do nearly all the work

Almost every entry is either a noun or a verb. Together they account for 99.7 percent of the dictionary. Everything else, the exclamations, pronouns, particles and a lone pair of numerals, would fit on a single page. A dictionary, it turns out, is mostly a warehouse of things and actions.

34,726 nouns and 11,445 verbs. Everything else adds up to 143 entries.

Entries by part of speech.

Four

There are more feminine words than masculine ones

Somali nouns carry gender, and the balance tips feminine. Where the dictionary marks it, feminine nouns outnumber masculine ones by several thousand, with a smaller group that works either way. Verbs and function words have no gender to show, which is why a quarter of the book leaves the field blank.

18,410 feminine nouns against 13,934 masculine, plus 2,226 that go both ways.

Gender of nouns, where the dictionary records it.

Five

Every verb fits one of four molds

Somali verbs are famously orderly. Each one belongs to a single conjugation class, and the first class is by far the largest. The dictionary also notes whether a verb takes an object, and here Somali leans intransitive: more verbs describe something happening than something being done to a thing.

Class I holds 4,170 verbs. Intransitive verbs (6,528) outnumber transitive ones (4,821).

Six

One small word, seventeen meanings

Here is my favorite find. Most words mean one thing, and nearly twenty thousand entries have a single sense. But a few carry an entire world. The champion is bax, a three letter verb with seventeen recorded senses. It can mean to leave, to die, to sprout, to rise like the sun, to catch fire, to be sold, and a dozen more, depending on the little particle sitting beside it.

19,735 words have exactly one sense. The verb bax has 17.

    Seven

    The same word, spelled several ways

    Somali was standardized in writing only in 1972, and the dictionary quietly records the aftermath. Some words appear under a dozen or more spellings, each pointing back to one preferred form. Others are genuine repeats: the same spelling reused for unrelated meanings. The tiny word ah shows up sixteen separate times.

    maageernaan has 14 recorded spellings. In all, 4,436 headwords appear more than once.

    Eight

    Half the book is a set of signposts

    This is the finding that surprised me most. Only about half the entries actually define a word. The rest are redirects, cross references that send you to the real entry elsewhere. So the dictionary is two things at once: a book of meanings, and a map that ties every spelling and variant back to where the meaning lives.

    21,032 entries (45 percent) are redirects. Only 25,240 carry a definition.

    Defined entries against cross reference redirects.

    Nine

    Where the dictionary gets technical

    A small slice of entries wear a subject tag, and reading down the list feels like walking through a university: medicine, physics, mathematics, biology, chemistry, religion, geography, geology, botany and music. Medicine leads by a wide margin, which fits a dictionary built to carry Somali into every field of modern learning.

    Only 4.2 percent of entries are tagged by field, yet those tags span more than a dozen disciplines.

    The most common subject fields. The dictionary abbreviates these in Somali (daaw., fiis., xis. and so on).

    Ten

    And what it leaves blank

    No entry is required to be complete, and the empty fields tell their own story. Plurals are noted for only one word in ten. Synonyms and verb detail cover about a quarter each. The bones of every entry, its part of speech and page number, are always there, but the richer fields were filled in only where the editors had the material. It is an honest record of a huge manual effort.

    Part of speech is recorded for 100 percent of entries. Plurals for just 10 percent.
